    Choose a direction

    Answer these honestly before committing a year of preparation to one track.

    About you

    • Do I want this field, and can I say why in one specific sentence?
    • Do I do my best work alone or in a team, and with clients or with problems?
    • How much time will I trade for higher pay, and how much do I value job security?
    • Do I perform well under pressure and in competitive settings?
    • Am I a creative problem solver or a systematic one?

    About the work

    • Do I want to work on markets, on deals, or on the systems underneath both?
    • Do I prefer fast decisions with immediate feedback or deep research over months?
    • Do I want qualitative analysis or quantitative and technical work?
    • Do I want pay tied to performance or a predictable salary?
    • Which asset classes or products interest me? Ask this once you have real exposure.

    Habits that compound

    1. Start early and teach yourself. Coursework arrives after recruiting does. Interviewers are more forgiving of younger applicants who show initiative.
    2. Keep your options open. Any relevant experience counts; a smaller firm with real responsibility beats a famous name with none.
    3. Understand a role before you apply. Every interview tests whether you know what the job is.
    4. Keep your resume current and have several people review it each semester.
    5. Network with people close to you first. Upperclassmen and FQE alumni are easy to reach and underrated.
    6. Read daily. The Wall Street Journal (free for Baruch students) or the Financial Times, plus one newsletter for your track.
    7. Expect rejection. Students starting from a less advantaged position will hear no more often; the response that matters is the next application.
    8. Be yourself. Interviewers notice insincerity and buzzwords quickly.
    9. Be kind. Competitive environments reward people others want to work with.
    10. Keep an identity outside your career. Not every conversation needs to be about work or money.

    Questions every interviewer can ask

    These appear across quant, trading, and banking interviews. Prepare a short answer to each.

    • Walk me through your resume. (See Elevator Pitches.)
    • How do you follow the markets? Name your sources, then tell me a story from the past week.
    • What is the federal funds rate, and what does the Fed do when inflation runs hot?
    • Walk me through the three financial statements and how they connect.
    • Pitch me a stock, or a trade. State the thesis, the catalyst, the instrument, and what would prove you wrong.
    • You have $1,000,000 to invest. How would you allocate it? Tie the answer to the role, and avoid answers like all of it in one index.

    Quant firms add probability, mental math, and coding to this list. See Interview Prep and Practice Problems.

    Common mistakes

    The errors that cost Baruch students offers most often, from strategy down to etiquette.

    Misconceptions

    • Finance and accounting are not the same field. They overlap, but the principles and careers differ.
    • Finance is far more than the stock market, and quant is far more than trading.
    • "Quant requires a PhD." Trading, developer, and most research internships hire undergraduates; PhD-only postings are specific research tracks.
    • "I need finance experience first." HRT says most of its people joined with no prior finance experience. Reviewers look for independent quantitative work.
    • The role matters more than the logo. Relevant experience at a smaller firm usually beats unrelated work at a famous one.
    • Baruch is a strong value school, not a name that opens doors by itself. Expect to work harder for the same interviews, and use that work ethic.
    • You do not have to chase the most prestigious role. Pursue what fits your interests; for many people the most competitive paths are a poor bet once the time cost is counted.

    Strategy mistakes

    • Waiting for classes to teach you. Recruiting finishes before the relevant courses start.
    • Preparing for the wrong track. More LeetCode does not help a trader's mental math screen.
    • Applying unprepared. A failed assessment can bar you from a firm for months.
    • Treating the closing date as the deadline. Rolling review fills classes weeks earlier.
    • Too few applications. Expect to send many before an offer; numbers and consistency compound.
    • "Front office or nothing." Openness to adjacent roles produces experience that leads back to the target.
    • Sloppy resumes. The easiest part of the process to get right, and the most common reason for automatic rejection.
    • Vagueness. "I want to work in business" or "how do I get into quant?" signals that you have not done the research.
    • Not searching first. Look things up before asking; save conversations for what cannot be found online.

    Conduct

    • Be more than your field. Interviewers hire people they want to sit next to every day.
    • Work hard without becoming exhausting. Intensity that makes others uncomfortable backfires.
    • Be kind, especially to people earlier in the process. Someone you help today may help you later, and an offer does not make anyone better than their classmates.
    • Be a sponge. Professionals care more about your capacity to learn than about what you already know.
    • Be careful what you post. LinkedIn and forwarded group chats are public.

    Frequently asked questions

    I am a freshman. Where do I start?

    Read Orientation and Common Mistakes, join an FQE desk, and start the Foundation stage of the skill ladder.

    Am I too late?

    No. Whatever year you are in, there is a next cycle, a winter program, an off-cycle role, or a new-grad path. Start the next step this week.

    Does GPA matter?

    Yes, especially before you have experience. Many firms screen at 3.2 to 3.5, and some bank programs post higher minimums. If your GPA is lower, strengthen projects, competitions, and referrals.

    Do I need to be a math or finance major?

    No. Fortune reported that about 90% of Citadel's 2026 interns came from computer science, physics, or statistics backgrounds; the common thread is quantitative depth, not a particular major. For non-quant finance roles, students from every major break in. Choose a major where you will earn a strong GPA.

    Which FQE desk should I start on?

    Alternative Data is built for newer members; Research is for returning members. Otherwise choose the desk closest to the role you will apply for. See the desk map on the home page.

    Can I recruit for investment banking and quant at the same time?

    You can, but the calendars collide: banks fill many junior-summer seats during sophomore winter, while quant firms open in July and August. Pick a primary track. Bank sales and trading and strats overlap most with quant preparation.

    What if I miss a cycle?

    Apply to engineering roles at the same firms, bank sales and trading, quantitative risk, and the next winter program, then reapply the following summer with a stronger project.

    What will make me the most money?

    Quant trading and research currently pay undergraduates the most in finance. Pay is one factor; the work, the people, and the hours decide whether you stay long enough to benefit from it.

      The firms

      Market makers and proprietary trading firms

      Market makers quote prices at which they will both buy and sell, earn the spread, and manage the inventory that results. That liquidity lets retail and institutional investors enter and exit positions at fair prices. Proprietary trading firms trade only their own capital. Examples include Jane Street, Citadel Securities, Optiver, IMC, Susquehanna (SIG), Hudson River Trading, Jump Trading, DRW, Five Rings, Akuna Capital, and Flow Traders. They hire undergraduates directly, pay the highest intern salaries in finance, and test probability and mental math more heavily than any other employer.

      Systematic and quantitative hedge funds

      These funds manage outside capital with model-driven strategies. Two Sigma, D. E. Shaw, Citadel, AQR, and similar firms hire undergraduates into research, engineering, and analyst roles. Some research tracks, notably at Renaissance Technologies, hire almost exclusively from STEM PhD programs. See Hedge Funds for strategy types.

      Banks

      Banks hire quants into strats, structuring, quantitative research, and risk. Recruiting follows the summer analyst calendar rather than the quant firm calendar, so it opens earlier. Sales and trading roles overlap heavily with quant preparation.

      Exchanges, clearinghouses, and vendors

      CME Group, Cboe, Nasdaq, ICE, and data vendors such as Bloomberg hire engineers and quantitative analysts for product design, market data, and margin models. See Exchanges.

      Interview structure at quant firms

      1. Online assessment. Arrives within days of applying and expires quickly. Trading tracks test timed mental math and probability; Optiver's format of 80 arithmetic questions in 8 minutes with penalties for errors is the best known. Research and engineering tracks test coding on HackerRank or CodeSignal.
      2. Phone or video screens. One or two rounds of 30 to 60 minutes covering probability, brainteasers, and live coding. Think aloud; interviewers grade the reasoning, not only the answer.
      3. Final round. Three to five interviews, often including a market-making or betting game for traders and a deeper design round for engineers, plus "why this firm."

      A failed assessment can bar you from reapplying for months. Optiver's posting set an eight-month window for the 2027 cycle (Extern). Apply when you are prepared, not merely when the portal opens.

      For bank and fund interview formats, see Interview Structure.

      Options and volatility primer

      The minimum options knowledge a market-making interviewer expects, with the formulas you should be able to write from memory.

      Contracts and payoffs

      A call gives the right, not the obligation, to buy the underlying at strike \(K\) on or before expiry \(T\); a put gives the right to sell. At expiry, with underlying price \(S_T\):

      \[ \text{Call payoff} = \max(S_T - K,\, 0), \qquad \text{Put payoff} = \max(K - S_T,\, 0). \]

      An option is in the money when exercising now would pay, at the money when the strike equals the spot price, and out of the money otherwise. American options can be exercised any time before expiry; European options only at expiry.

      Put-call parity

      For European options on a non-dividend-paying stock with continuously compounded rate \(r\), a long call and a short put with the same strike and expiry replicate a forward. Any deviation is an arbitrage, and interviewers ask you to find it.

      \[ C - P = S_0 - K e^{-rT}. \]

      Black-Scholes

      Under the Black-Scholes assumptions (lognormal prices, constant volatility \(\sigma\), continuous hedging, no frictions), the European call price is

      \[ C = S_0\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2), \qquad d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + \left(r + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}, \]

      where \(N(\cdot)\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. A useful approximation for an at-the-money option with small \(r\) and \(T\):

      \[ C_{\text{ATM}} \approx 0.4\, S_0\, \sigma \sqrt{T}. \]

      The Greeks

      Volatility

      Realized volatility is the annualized standard deviation of observed returns. Implied volatility is the \(\sigma\) that makes the model price equal the market price. A market maker who is long an option and delta-hedges profits, approximately, when realized volatility exceeds the implied volatility paid:

      \[ \text{Daily P\&L} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma\, S^2 \left(\sigma_{\text{realized}}^2 - \sigma_{\text{implied}}^2\right)\Delta t. \]

      Plotting implied volatility against strike gives the skew or smile; adding expiry gives the volatility surface. Equity index options usually show higher implied volatility for low strikes because investors pay up for crash protection.

      Go deeper with Natenberg's Option Volatility and Pricing, Sinclair's Volatility Trading, and the Options Industry Council. The Options and Commodities desk builds these models every semester.

      Practice problems

      Classic questions in the style of quant phone screens. Solve each aloud before opening the solution.

      1. How many rolls of a fair die do you expect until the first six?

      The number of rolls is geometric with success probability \(p = 1/6\), so

      \[ \mathbb{E}[N] = \frac{1}{p} = 6. \]

      Follow-up an interviewer may ask: the expected number of rolls to see every face at least once is \(6\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{6}\right) = 14.7\).

      2. Two cards are drawn without replacement from a standard deck. What is the probability both are aces?
      \[ \frac{4}{52}\cdot\frac{3}{51} = \frac{12}{2652} = \frac{1}{221} \approx 0.45\%. \]
      3. What is the expected value of the maximum of two fair dice?

      \(P(\max \le k) = (k/6)^2\), so \(P(\max = k) = \dfrac{k^2 - (k-1)^2}{36} = \dfrac{2k-1}{36}\). Then

      \[ \mathbb{E}[\max] = \sum_{k=1}^{6} k\,\frac{2k-1}{36} = \frac{161}{36} \approx 4.47. \]
      4. How many coin flips do you expect until two heads in a row?

      Let \(E_0\) be the expected flips from the start and \(E_1\) the expected flips after one head.

      \[ E_0 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}E_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}E_0, \qquad E_1 = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}(0) + \tfrac{1}{2}E_0. \]

      Solving gives \(E_0 = 6\). The same setup for head-then-tail gives 4; interviewers often ask why the two differ.

      5. Make me a market on the sum of two dice.

      Fair value is 7. A reasonable opening market is 6.5 bid, 7.5 offer. Expect the interviewer to trade against you, reveal information (for example, "one die shows a 6"), and ask you to update: the new fair value is \(6 + 3.5 = 9.5\). Keep the spread tight when you are confident, widen it when you face adverse selection, and track your position as you trade.

      6. A call trades at 10, a put at 4, both strike 100 and one year to expiry. The stock is at 100 and rates are zero. Is there an arbitrage?

      Put-call parity with \(r = 0\) requires \(C - P = S - K = 0\), but \(C - P = 6\). The call is rich relative to the put. Sell the call, buy the put, and buy the stock with borrowed money. The net outlay is 100 - 10 + 4 = 94. At expiry the package is worth exactly 100 whatever the stock does, so after repaying the loan you keep 6, before costs.

      7. You may roll a die once and be paid its face value, or roll a second time and take the second value. What is the game worth?

      Keep the first roll if it beats the expected value of a fresh roll, 3.5, so keep 4, 5, or 6.

      \[ \mathbb{E} = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{4+5+6}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 3.5 = 2.5 + 1.75 = 4.25. \]
      8. Estimate \(\sqrt{50}\) to two decimal places without a calculator.

      Expand around 49: \(\sqrt{49 + 1} \approx 7 + \dfrac{1}{2\cdot 7} = 7.0714\), so 7.07. Linear approximations like this appear constantly in trading arithmetic.

      9. Write a function that returns the median of a stream of numbers after each insertion.

      Keep a max-heap of the lower half and a min-heap of the upper half, rebalancing so their sizes differ by at most one. Each insertion is \(O(\log n)\) and each median query is \(O(1)\).

      import heapq

class RunningMedian:
    def __init__(self):
        self.low = []   # max-heap via negated values
        self.high = []  # min-heap

    def add(self, x):
        heapq.heappush(self.low, -x)
        heapq.heappush(self.high, -heapq.heappop(self.low))
        if len(self.high) > len(self.low):
            heapq.heappush(self.low, -heapq.heappop(self.high))

    def median(self):
        if len(self.low) > len(self.high):
            return -self.low[0]
        return (-self.low[0] + self.high[0]) / 2

      More problems: Brainstellar, QuantGuide, and Zhou's Practical Guide. The Puzzles and Competitions desk runs timed sets during the semester.

      Resumes

      Reviewers spend seconds on a first pass. A clean, consistent, one-page resume already beats most of the pile.

      Principles

      • Uniformity wins. Most rejected resumes fail on sloppy, inconsistent formatting.
      • Quantify honestly. Use real numbers you can explain. An invented figure collapses under one follow-up question.
      • Everything is fair game. Be ready to walk through every line in depth. Never lie.
      • Skip the personal statement and soft-skill lists. If anyone could claim it, it says nothing.
      • Keep a short interests line with specific, memorable hobbies. They start conversations.
      • Add a Projects section for FQE work, research, and publications.

      Technical resume layout

      Use one page, a single column, and a standard font (Times New Roman, Garamond, Calibri, Arial, or Helvetica) at 10 to 12 points. Jake's Resume on Overleaf is the common LaTeX template for quant and engineering roles. Order the sections:

      1. Education: GPA, expected graduation, and relevant coursework (probability, statistics, linear algebra, stochastic processes, data structures).
      2. Experience: internships, research, and jobs, most recent first.
      3. Projects: your FQE desk project with a repository link, stating the question, the method, and a number.
      4. Competitions and awards.
      5. Skills: only languages and tools you could be tested on live today.

      Finance resumes follow the bank format instead: education first, then experience with transaction or deal specifics.

      Weak:   Worked on an options pricing project for FQE.

      Elevator pitches

      Your answer to "walk me through your resume." Your story, more than your GPA, is what interviewers remember.

      Structure

      1. Background: school, major, and year.
      2. The specific experience that pulled you toward markets or engineering.
      3. How you progressed: courses, FQE desks, projects, internships.
      4. Why this role and why this firm.
      5. One memorable detail from outside work.

      Adapt the structure; cut what does not serve the story.

      Tips

      • Length: 90 seconds to two minutes.
      • Specificity: "I have always liked math" or "I have always been interested in how money moves" says nothing. "I wanted to know why my options model mispriced short-dated puts" invites a follow-up you can win.
      • Relevance: include only experiences that lead to the role.
      • Tailoring: keep a version for each track you recruit for.
      • Delivery: script it, then rehearse until it sounds like conversation. Show that you want to be there.
      • Weaknesses: leave them for when you are asked.
      • Show, do not claim: demonstrate qualities through stories rather than calling yourself a leader.
      • Workshop it with FQE members and mentors until the most distinctive parts stand out.

      Networking

      Networking is how you learn what a job is really like and how people at a firm come to know your name before your application arrives.

      Who to contact, in order

      1. FQE members and alumni who have interned at the firms you target.
      2. Baruch alumni at those firms; search LinkedIn by school and company.
      3. People who share a club, hometown, prior employer, or unusual interest with you.
      4. Speakers and recruiters you met at events, followed up within 48 hours.

      Referrals matter most at banks and large technology companies, and least at the largest quant firms, which screen most applicants through a standardized assessment. Conversations still help everywhere: they sharpen your "why this firm" answer and surface roles before they post.

      The coffee chat

      A 15- to 20-minute call. Prepare three or four questions you could not answer from the firm's website, and let the other person do most of the talking.

      • How did you choose your team, and what does a typical week look like?
      • What separates the strongest interns on your desk?
      • What do you wish you had learned before you started?
      • Is there anyone else you would suggest I speak with?

      Avoid asking for a referral in the first conversation, and avoid questions a quick search would answer. The Extern coffee chat guide has more questions.

      Interview structure

      Most processes move from an automated screen to short live rounds to a final round with several interviewers.

      Recorded video assessments

      Larger banks and companies often screen with recorded video platforms such as HireVue. The invitation usually arrives within a day or two of applying and expires within a few days. Each question appears on screen with about 30 seconds to prepare and one or two retries.

      • Use a quiet, well-lit room with a plain background, and test your connection first.
      • Dress in business professional attire and look at the camera, not the screen.
      • Expect mostly behavioral questions; technical questions, if any, are basic.
      • Practice on HireVue's practice tool or the Starr Career Development Center's mock interview module.

      Online assessments

      Quant and technology firms screen with timed tests instead: mental math, probability, numerical reasoning, or coding. See Roles and Firms for formats.

      Live rounds

      1. HR or recruiter call: a short screen of interest, logistics, and communication.
      2. First round: about 30 minutes with one or two interviewers. Depending on the firm it is a technical screen or a fit conversation; some firms run several rounds before the final.
      3. Final round or superday: three to five back-to-back interviews with people at different levels. Senior people tend to hold conversational interviews looking for someone they would mentor; analysts and associates tend to ask harder technical and behavioral questions.

      The Extern superday guide covers final-round formats and firm rules on AI use.

      Question types

      Behavioral, logical reasoning, case, technical, and your own questions at the end. Prepare each separately.

      Behavioral

      Every industry asks about your background. Structure each answer with a clear opening, specific detail, and a conclusion; a common frame is situation, task, action, result. Specific answers show real experience; vague ones read as invented. Build a set of five or six stories that you can adapt to most questions.

      Candidacy

      • Why this role and this firm?
      • Why should we hire you?
      • What do you know about what we do?

      Attributes

      • Your greatest strength and weakness?
      • How would teammates describe you?
      • What are you most proud of?

      Scenarios

      • A time you failed and what you changed.
      • A conflict on a team and how it ended.
      • A decision made with incomplete information.

      Logical reasoning and brainteasers

      Deductive questions give premises and ask for the conclusion that follows; identify the assumptions and apply valid inference. Inductive questions give examples and ask for the rule. Ask clarifying questions first and think aloud; interviewers weigh the process over the final answer. Market-making interviews add probability, conditional reasoning, confidence intervals, adverse selection, and optimization. See Practice Problems.

      Case studies

      Consulting firms use cases most, but banks, funds, and trading firms use them too: market sizing (Fermi estimates), profitability, investment decisions, and business problems. Frameworks such as profitability trees, Porter's Five Forces, SWOT, and a two-by-two matrix are starting points; build a structure for the specific problem. Practice with the case libraries McKinsey, BCG, Bain, and Oliver Wyman publish, and Marc Cosentino's Case in Point.

      Technical

      Technical questions test the concrete skills of the job: probability and coding for quant, accounting and valuation for banking, credit for lending. Learn the concepts rather than memorizing guide answers, because interviewers change the numbers. Practice aloud, ask for missing information before answering, answer only what was asked, and when you do not know, say so and reason toward an answer.

      Your questions at the end

      Always have questions. Prepare at least three open-ended ones, ideally about the interviewer's own path or work. Researching your interviewers shows diligence; use what you learn to ask good questions rather than to recite their biography.

      Preparation and practices

      What to do in the weeks before, the morning of, during, and after an interview.

      Before

      Weeks before

      • Rehearse every question type for your track.
      • Schedule mock interviews with FQE members and mentors.
      • For quant roles, drill mental math and probability daily.

      Days before

      • Research your interviewers if you know their names; do not contact them.
      • Tell anyone at the firm who helped you that your interview is coming, and thank them.
      • Practice speaking aloud at a steady, confident pace.

      Morning of

      • Review rates, the major indices, company news, and the macro headlines of the day.
      • Arrive 15 to 20 minutes early, or log in early for video.
      • Look calm even if you are not.

      During

      • Open with energy and confidence; maintain posture and natural eye contact.
      • Speak clearly and at a measured pace. A deliberate pause reads better than filler words.
      • Keep a conversational tone. It makes behavioral answers warmer and technical answers sound understood rather than memorized.
      • Engage with everything the interviewer says; follow a story with a question or comment.
      • Stay on the question and stay composed when it gets hard.
      • Make it clear that this firm is where you want to be.

      After

      • Send a personalized thank-you note to each interviewer.
      • If you missed a question, a brief follow-up with the correct reasoning can help.
      • Wait about two weeks before asking about status; HR timelines run long.
      • Ask for feedback, knowing not every firm can give it.
      • Write down what went well and what to fix, then keep applying until you sign.
      • Judge offers on the work, the team, and the culture as well as pay and prestige.

      How finance fits together

      Before choosing a career, understand the system: who has capital, who needs it, and which institutions sit between them.

      The flow of capital

      Households, companies, and governments with surplus cash save and invest it. Companies and governments that need capital borrow or issue shares. Banks, asset managers, insurers, exchanges, and trading firms stand in between: they underwrite securities, lend, make markets, manage portfolios, and clear trades. The central bank sets the price of short-term money, which feeds into every other price in the system. Robert Shiller's Financial Markets course walks through each institution in depth.

      Three ways to sort careers

      Sell side and buy side

      The sell side, mainly investment banks, creates and sells securities and services: advisory, underwriting, market making, and research. The buy side, made up of hedge funds, private equity, asset managers, pensions, and endowments, buys those services and invests capital. Buy-side seats often recruit from the sell side, and which sell-side seat matters: private equity prefers investment bankers for their transaction experience, sector hedge funds prefer equity research analysts or industry specialists, and quant funds look for traders and engineers with the right technical background. Many careers, including trading firms, exchanges, and corporate finance, fit neither label.

      Front office

      • Investment banking division. Coverage groups own client relationships within an industry (technology, healthcare, financial institutions). Product groups execute a type of transaction (M&A, leveraged finance, equity and debt capital markets, restructuring).
      • Sales and trading. Salespeople manage institutional client relationships; traders make markets and manage risk in rates, credit, FX, commodities, and equities. Strats build the desk's pricing models and tools.

      • Asset and wealth management. Invests for institutions and wealthy individuals.

      Middle office

      • Risk management measures and limits market, credit, liquidity, and operational risk across the firm.
      • Compliance keeps the firm within the law. Subteams handle regulatory reporting, financial crime (fraud, money laundering, bribery), trade surveillance, and the control room that separates public and private information. Its role grew sharply after 2008.

      Back office

      • Operations settles and processes what the front office executes, globally and under strict regulatory control. Interviews are mostly fit-based; genuine interest in the role matters more than technical depth.
      • Human resources recruits, trains, evaluates, and compensates staff.
      • The finance function produces the firm's own financial reporting and planning; it is closer to accounting than to markets. See Corporate Finance.

      Corporate banking

      Corporate bankers lend to large companies and governments and sell them related services such as cash management. The two core products are the revolver, a credit line the borrower can draw and repay repeatedly up to a limit, much like a corporate credit card, and the term loan, a lump sum repaid on a schedule and often used for capital spending, acquisitions, or recapitalizations. In a syndicated loan, a bank may act as lead arranger (underwrites and syndicates), agent (collects and distributes payments among lenders), or administrator (processing and reporting). Teams are organized by industry.

      Commercial banking

      Commercial banks serve small and mid-sized businesses with loans, credit lines, and cash management. Lending decisions rest mainly on the borrower's creditworthiness. Regional banks compete on local relationships and tailored terms.

      Retail banking

      Retail banks serve individuals with checking and savings accounts, cards, mortgages, and personal loans, earning the spread between loan and deposit rates plus fees. Most activity has moved online, and branch roles are shrinking.

      Recruiting

      Expect questions on credit analysis, creditworthiness, loan structures, and why a given client would choose one debt product over another, along with the accounting and financial statement questions common in banking interviews. Hours and pay are lower than in investment banking, with better balance.

      Where FQE skills fit: credit risk modeling, loan pricing analytics, and the quantitative risk teams that support lending books.

      Go deeper

      • Sell Side Handbook for a corporate banking guide.
      • Bank student portals for credit analyst and corporate banking programs.

      Hedge funds

      Actively managed pools of capital for institutions and wealthy investors, with wide latitude to use leverage, short selling, and derivatives.

      The name comes from hedging: pairing long and short positions so returns depend on the manager's view rather than the overall market. Lighter regulation than mutual funds allows concentrated and unconventional strategies. A growing share of hedge fund capital runs on quantitative and systematic models.

      Recruiting

      Undergraduate seats are scarce and every fund recruits differently. A few large firms run formal internships (Citadel, Bridgewater, Point72, and the quant funds such as Two Sigma and D. E. Shaw); interviews can range from market views to open-ended reasoning, and Bridgewater is known for debate-style group rounds. Many funds hire only experienced candidates from banking, research, or trading, and sector funds hire specialists. Research every fund's process individually.

      Where FQE skills fit: quantitative research, data science, and engineering at systematic funds are the most direct undergraduate path. See Roles and Firms.

      • Wall Street Oasis for fund-level interview reports; read critically.
      • Roger Lowenstein, When Genius Failed, and Michael Lewis, The Big Short.

      Private equity and venture capital

      Investors that buy stakes in private companies, improve them, and sell them years later.

      Traditional private equity

      A private equity firm raises a fund from institutions and wealthy investors, buys companies (often with substantial borrowed money in a leveraged buyout), works to increase their value through operational changes, add-on acquisitions, or cost reductions, and exits through a sale or IPO, usually over three to seven years.

      The fund is a partnership. The general partner manages the fund and its companies day to day and bears its obligations. Limited partners supply most of the capital and can lose no more than they invested.

      Common deal types

      • Buy-and-build: acquire a platform company, then bolt on smaller acquisitions.
      • Take-private: buy a public company and delist it.
      • Carve-out: buy a division from a larger company and run it independently.
      • Transformational acquisitions: deals that change what the company does.

      Venture capital

      Venture capital funds back startups in exchange for equity, adding advice and networks along with capital. Most investments fail; a few large outcomes return the fund. Undergraduates now enter through venture fellow programs and internships, and applications often require essays, case studies, or pitches.

      Corporate venture capital

      Corporate venture arms invest a company's own money in startups that serve its strategy: entering new markets, acquiring technology, or hedging against disruption. They tend to be more cautious than independent funds because strategic mistakes can affect the parent company.

      Growth equity

      Growth investors buy minority stakes in established, growing companies to fund expansion into new markets, products, or acquisitions. It sits between venture capital and buyouts.

      Recruiting

      Most private equity associates come from two years of investment banking, recruited through structured cycles. Undergraduate programs exist but are rare; Bain Capital's sophomore Investors of Tomorrow event is one. Interviews test LBO modeling and investment judgment.

      Where FQE skills fit: data science and portfolio analytics teams at large firms, and technical diligence at technology-focused funds. The direct path runs through banking.

      Go deeper

      • Mergers and Inquisitions for PE recruiting and LBO modeling.
      • 10X EBITDA for concise PE technical guides.
      • Bryan Burrough, Barbarians at the Gate.

      Asset management

      Firms and institutions that pool capital and invest it to meet their owners' goals, risk tolerance, and time horizon.

      Asset managers learn each client's objectives, build a strategy to match, manage the portfolio, and report on it. Some institutions set the asset allocation in house and hire outside managers to pick securities within each sleeve.

      Processes vary widely by firm and role. Know the firm's holdings and philosophy, and expect asset allocation questions for investment roles. Large managers such as BlackRock, Vanguard, Fidelity, and J.P. Morgan Asset Management run structured analyst programs.

      Real estate

      Real estate is both a career and an asset class that appears across banking, investing, and lending.

      Commercial real estate

      CRE professionals raise capital, screen and acquire properties, develop or reposition them, manage them, and sell them. Acquisitions runs diligence, negotiation, and closing; asset management maximizes value after purchase through budgets, leasing, and maintenance.

      Sectors

      • Traditional: office, multifamily, retail, industrial, hotel, single-family
      • Alternative: data centers, self-storage, senior living, life sciences, cell towers, manufactured housing

      Strategies, by risk

      • Core: stabilized, fully leased
      • Core-plus: stable with room to raise net operating income
      • Value-add: needs repositioning
      • Opportunistic: heavy redevelopment or ground-up

      REITs

      Real estate investment trusts own, operate, or finance income-producing property and let investors own real estate without buying buildings. Equity REITs earn rent, mortgage REITs earn interest on loans and mortgage-backed securities, and hybrids do both. They may be publicly traded, public but non-traded, or private. Funds from operations (FFO) is the standard performance measure.

      Housing GSEs

      Government-sponsored enterprises keep mortgage credit flowing by buying loans from lenders, pooling them into mortgage-backed securities, and selling those to investors. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac buy conventional loans; Ginnie Mae guarantees pools of government-insured loans (FHA, VA, USDA). Their agency bonds carry implicit, not explicit, government backing and yield slightly more than Treasuries.

      Recruiting

      Know cap rates, discounted cash flow, replacement cost, and comparables; follow recent deals in the sectors you target. GSE roles require fixed income and mortgage-market knowledge.

      Where FQE skills fit: mortgage and MBS prepayment modeling at GSEs, mortgage REITs, and fixed income desks. The Fixed Income and Rates desk is the natural preparation.

      Go deeper

      • A.CRE (Adventures in CRE) for free real estate modeling training.
      • Sam Zell, Am I Being Too Subtle?

      Commodities trading

      Buying, moving, and selling physical raw materials, and trading the derivatives written on them.

      Asset classes

      Agricultural (grains, livestock, softs such as coffee and cotton), energy (crude, products, natural gas, power), metals (precious and industrial), chemicals and fertilizers, raw materials such as timber, and environmental products such as carbon credits and renewable energy certificates.

      Physical and paper trading

      Physical

      Traders own the commodity and earn by moving it across time, place, and form: storage, shipping, blending, and delivery. The work involves logistics, quality, trade regulation, and negotiation.

      Paper

      Traders use futures, options, and swaps to hedge or speculate without taking delivery. The work is model-driven, and many traders come from mathematics backgrounds. Producers' paper arms trade on the market intelligence the physical business generates.

      Employers

      • Agricultural merchants (the ABCDs): ADM, Bunge, Cargill, and Louis Dreyfus.
      • Independent merchants: Trafigura, Vitol, Glencore, Mercuria, Gunvor.
      • Oil and gas majors: ExxonMobil, Shell, BP, TotalEnergies, Chevron, and others run rotational trader programs through risk, logistics, shipping, and trading.
      • Banks, hedge funds, and trading firms with commodity desks.

      Recruiting

      Physical roles test macroeconomics, seasonality, supply and demand, and negotiation; energy superdays often include a case study or mock trading. Paper trading interviews increasingly resemble market-maker interviews.

      Where FQE skills fit: paper trading, quantitative analysis, and risk. The Options and Commodities desk covers futures curves and options on commodities.

      Go deeper

      • Trafigura, Commodities Demystified, the standard introduction.
      • The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission's Energy Primer.
      • Javier Blas and Jack Farchy, The World for Sale.
      • CME Group Education.

      Exchanges and clearinghouses

      The venues where securities and derivatives trade, and the institutions that guarantee the trades settle.

      Exchanges

      Exchanges match buyers and sellers, design and list new products such as futures contracts, and sell market data and analytics (CME's FedWatch tool, which infers rate expectations from futures prices, is a familiar example).

      Exchanges by type
      TypeExamples
      Stock exchangesNew York Stock Exchange, Nasdaq, London Stock Exchange
      Futures and commoditiesCME Group, Intercontinental Exchange (ICE)
      OptionsCboe, Nasdaq PHLX, NYSE Arca, MIAX, Eurex

      Most trading is electronic, though some options floors remain for certain block trades.

      Clearinghouses

      A clearinghouse becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, so a trader's counterparty risk is the clearinghouse rather than an unknown firm. It protects itself with margin:

      • Initial margin is posted when a position opens.
      • Variation margin is paid daily as positions lose value.
      • Netting offsets a member's longs and shorts in the same contract, reducing exposure and collateral.

      Where FQE skills fit: margin and risk models, product design, market data engineering, and low-latency matching systems. Exchange experience transfers well to trading firms.

      Go deeper

      • CME Group Education and the Options Industry Council.
      • Larry Harris, Trading and Exchanges.

      Corporate finance

      The teams that manage a company's own money: planning, reporting, cash, tax, investors, and acquisitions.

      Corporate finance manages a company from the inside to maximize its value, while financial services firms sell products to outside clients. Hours are usually better than in banking or trading, and large technology companies pay the most, which makes their finance roles competitive. Many companies hire new graduates into rotational Financial Leadership Development Programs (FLDPs).

      FP&A interviews are mostly behavioral with some financial statement questions. Corporate development interviews resemble banking interviews.

      Where FQE skills fit: treasury hedging and risk, forecasting models, and the analytics and automation work that finance teams increasingly hire engineers for.

      Go deeper

      • FLDP postings on Starr Search (Handshake) and company careers pages.
      • Annual reports and 10-K filings on SEC EDGAR, which show how a company's finance team reports results.

      Accounting

      Recording, reporting, and verifying financial transactions. Careers divide into public, private, and government accounting.

      Public accounting

      Public accounting firms serve outside clients with audit, tax, and advisory services. Careers follow a structured ladder toward partnership, auditors travel to clients, and deadlines are tight because public companies' filings depend on the work. The CPA license is required to advance.

      Audit

      Associates plan and perform audits, test controls and balances, and give assurance that financial statements are fairly presented. Audit offers the widest exit options into industry. Peak season follows fiscal year-ends.

      Tax

      Associates prepare and review returns and advise clients on compliance and planning. Starting pay can exceed audit. Work peaks from January through April.

      Firms: the Big 4 (Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG), then national firms such as Grant Thornton, RSM, BDO, Crowe, and CBIZ.

      Private accounting

      Private accountants work in house for one company, recording transactions and preparing internal statements that external auditors later review. Roles are stable, travel is rare, and the career path is less structured. Many CPAs move here after a few years in public accounting.

      Government accounting

      Agencies such as the IRS, SEC, Social Security Administration, the Federal Reserve, and state and local governments employ accountants for tax administration, filing review, benefits, and budgets. Pay trails the private sector, but hours are predictable and pensions are generous.

      The CPA

      Since 2024 the exam has three core sections (auditing, financial accounting and reporting, and regulation) plus one discipline section of the candidate's choice. New York requires 150 semester hours of education for licensure along with experience requirements. Confirm current rules with the state board before planning coursework.

      Where FQE skills fit: audit data analytics, automation, and forensic accounting increasingly hire people who can code. Quant overlap is otherwise limited.

      Go deeper

      • AICPA, This Way to CPA for exam and licensure requirements by state.
      • Extern, Big 4 internships for program timing.
      • PwC Destination CPA, a sophomore program tied to an internship offer.
      • Baruch's accounting societies and Starr Career Development Center, which run the strongest on-campus pipelines for this path.
      • Big 4 careers pages (Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG) for program dates and eligibility.

      Management consulting

      Outside advisers who help companies solve strategic and operational problems, recruited through case interviews.

      Consultants work in small teams on engagements lasting weeks to months: market entry, pricing, cost reduction, operations, technology transformation, and due diligence for private equity. The top strategy firms are McKinsey, Boston Consulting Group, and Bain (MBB); the Big 4 run large consulting practices; and many boutiques specialize by industry or function.

      Case interviews

      A case presents a business problem and tests structured thinking, quantitative reasoning, and communication. Common types include profitability, market entry, market sizing (practice Fermi estimates), and investment cases. Frameworks such as profitability trees, Porter's Five Forces, and internal-versus-external factors are starting points, not scripts; interviewers reward a structure built for the specific problem.

      Recruiting

      Undergraduate recruiting has moved earlier, and several firms now recruit for the junior summer during sophomore year. Bain's sophomore programs and BCG's Embark are entry points.

      Where FQE skills fit: analytics practices, quantitative market sizing, and data-driven due diligence. Consulting is a common landing spot for members who want breadth over depth.

      Go deeper

      • Extern, consulting internships.
      • Marc Cosentino, Case in Point, and the practice cases each firm publishes on its careers site.
      • Firm careers pages for program dates and eligibility, which change every cycle.

      Adjacent and related industries

      Rating agencies, regulators, service providers, and actuaries sit next to the financial system and hire many of the same skills.

      Rating agencies

      Agencies rate the creditworthiness of companies, governments, and securities, from corporate bonds to asset-backed securities. Rating analysts assign ratings; macro analysts connect economic trends to issuers; many agencies also sell data and research. S&P Global Ratings, Moody's, and Fitch hold roughly 95% of the market.

      Regulators

      Prudential regulators such as the Federal Reserve and the OCC supervise the safety of banks and insurers. Market regulators such as the SEC, CFTC, and FINRA police fraud and manipulation. Pay trails the private sector, offset by mission, benefits, stability, and balance.

      Financial service providers

      Data, analytics, and software vendors (Bloomberg, FactSet, S&P Global Market Intelligence, MSCI, and many fintech firms) hire analysts, engineers, data scientists, product managers, and salespeople. Interviews test knowledge of the firm's products and the clients who buy them.

      Actuarial science

      Actuaries price and reserve for uncertain future events in insurance, pensions, and benefits, using probability and statistics. Credentials come through exams: the Society of Actuaries for life, health, retirement, and investments, and the Casualty Actuarial Society for property and casualty.

      Where FQE skills fit: credit and structured-finance modeling at agencies, surveillance analytics at regulators, engineering at vendors, and the probability foundation that actuarial exams build on.

      Go deeper

      • Be an Actuary, SOA, and CAS.
      • Federal Reserve Bank of New York, SEC, and FINRA careers pages.
      • Built In for fintech roles.
